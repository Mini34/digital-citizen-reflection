Ten-minute session

A news post worth checking

Imagine a friend sends a cropped screenshot saying city buses will be free next month. This example is fictional. You want to help friends with transport costs, so you are tempted to forward it. What would you check first? The screenshot has no date and no original announcement. Digital Citizen Reflection helps us describe that habit, compare relevant evidence, and choose a realistic change. Today I will show the four steps, then you can reflect privately. You can use a fictional example throughout.

Evidence and its limits

The activity uses original sources and puts their limits beside the findings. WHO reported signs of problematic social media use in eleven percent of the young people surveyed in 2022. The study covered almost 280,000 participants aged eleven, thirteen, and fifteen across forty-four countries and regions. That gives useful population context. It cannot diagnose one person or define a safe number of minutes for everyone. UNESCO's media literacy resources offer another kind of evidence: guidance for thinking critically about information. Guidance can help us decide how to check a post, but it does not establish whether that post is true.

The four-step framework

The same four steps work across attention, privacy, news, and AI tools. Observe means describing something you actually do, and when it happens. Evaluate means looking at a source, its date, who it covers, and its limitations. Identify means naming a risk while keeping sight of a benefit you value. Act means choosing one realistic change, a way to check what happens, and a date to review it. The page suggests a review seven days ahead, but you can change it. The aim is a useful action plan rather than a score or a judgment about the person.

Observe: the news-sharing habit

Our fictional post has no date or original link. We can describe that without calling it true or false. In Observe, I select News and information and load the fictional example. If the question about what I notice feels difficult, Help me answer offers smaller prompts. For example: what changes, if anything, afterward? I can skip, say I am not sure, or report no change. A draft uses only the words I supply; I review it before using it.

Evaluate: what supports the claim?

In Evaluate, the relevant sources help me think about checking information. I can see the publisher and date, then open the population and limitation section. UNESCO's guidance asks me to engage critically with information. It does not verify our fictional transport claim. I would still need a dated announcement from the transit agency, with the details and conditions. This is why comparing a finding with its limits matters. A trustworthy source can guide a process without answering every question about a particular post.

Identify: the risk and the benefit

Now I identify the risk: passing along a claim before I have evidence for it. That can mislead people making plans. The benefit is still worth keeping. I want to help friends find useful transport information. Naming both makes the change more realistic. I do not have to stop sharing news to improve how I share it. The form lets participants select a risk or describe their own, and it asks them to write the benefit they want to preserve.

Act: a change we can check

The change is specific: find a dated transit-agency announcement before forwarding the claim. If I cannot verify it, I will say that clearly. The check is also specific: for the next three news posts I consider sharing, note whether I found an original source. I can review that in seven days. The summary stays editable. I can download a text copy or print it. Answers remain temporary unless I enable Save on this device. Saving uses this browser only, so a shared device needs care. Clear reflection removes the draft and the current answers.

Your two-minute reflection

Take two minutes for your own reflection. Use the page, the worksheet, or your own notes. Pick one habit or a fictional example. Keep names and private details out. Name a risk and a benefit to preserve. Choose one small change and a check you can observe. You can keep this private and finish later.

Development lessons

Building the activity made source limits and privacy choices concrete. The helper waits for an invitation and leaves the participant in control. Testing also covered going back, recovering drafts, keyboard use, and blocked storage.

One change, then a review

The takeaway is a process you can repeat: observe, evaluate, identify, and act. Keep the benefit you value, try one realistic change, and review what actually happened.
